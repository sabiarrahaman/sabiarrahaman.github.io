About

Background and training

I grew up in West Bengal and am now a Marie Skłodowska-Curie PhD researcher in the Department of Physics & Astronomy at the University of Manchester, working with Professor Radha Boya. My work asks how the structure of water changes under extreme confinement, and how that structure then governs the way charge and mass move through the channel.

The applications sit close behind the physics: fluidic memristors that store and process information using ions rather than electrons, ion-gating, desalination, osmotic energy harvesting and flow sensing.

Before this I was a Commonwealth Split-site Scholar in the Department of Chemistry here, studying ion transport through angstrom-scale capillaries built from two-dimensional materials, and before that a research fellow at the Centre for Nano and Soft Matter Sciences in Bengaluru, working on framework materials for supercapacitors and batteries. That earlier line of work still runs alongside the nanofluidics, and the two are steadily converging.

I expect to finish in September 2027. I am always glad to hear from people working on confined transport, ionic memory, or framework materials for energy storage — do get in touch.

Water changes when the walls close in

Channels built from two-dimensional crystals can be made one or two molecular layers tall. At that scale water can no longer arrange itself the way it does in bulk, and ion transport through the channel departs sharply from what continuum theory predicts.

I fabricate these angstrom-scale capillaries and measure what passes through them, trying to connect the structure of the confined liquid to the charge and mass transport we observe.

Memory made from ions instead of electrons

A nanofluidic channel can remember its own history. Drive ions through it and the conductance depends on what came before — the defining behaviour of a memristor, and the same physical currency the brain uses to signal.

I work on the devices themselves and, with colleagues, on mapping where the field is heading. Our review of nanofluidic ionic memory appeared in Nature Reviews Materials in 2026.

  • Fluidic memristors
  • Ion gating
  • Neuromorphic devices
  • Unconventional computing

Framework materials that hold charge

Covalent organic frameworks and two-dimensional metal–organic frameworks have ordered, tunable pores, which makes them unusually good places to ask how charge is actually stored. We have shown how the balance between capacitive and diffusive storage shifts as these films grow thicker, and built nitrogen-rich frameworks that stay stable over long cycling.

Earlier work on lanthanide coordination polymers produced flexible micro-supercapacitors and two patents, and morphology engineering by electrochemical methods raised capacitance by close to an order of magnitude.

  • Covalent organic frameworks
  • 2D MOFs
  • Supercapacitors
  • Lithium-ion batteries
